# Mis-Specified Consent: Toward a Shared Decision Model in Clinical Care

## Abstract

“Informed consent” is often treated as a procedural requirement in medicine. Yet in practice, it frequently fails—not due to lack of data, but due to a deeper structural issue: the physician and patient are solving fundamentally different optimization problems. This essay argues that clinical decision-making should be reframed as a problem of **loss function alignment**, where outcomes, probabilities, and—critically—patient-specific weights are made explicit. Without this, consent becomes nominal rather than real.

---

## 1. The Illusion of Informed Consent

Modern clinical practice assumes that if risks and benefits are disclosed, consent is achieved. But disclosure alone does not guarantee understanding, nor does it ensure alignment between physician intent and patient values.

Consider a typical exchange:

* “We can proceed surgically or manage this conservatively.”
* “Which would you prefer?”

This question presumes that the patient has a stable preference. But **preference without structured information is undefined**. Patients cannot meaningfully choose between options they do not fully understand—especially when terminology (e.g., “conservative management”) is interpreted differently across clinical and lay contexts.

Thus, what is labeled “consent” is often **decision-making under semantic ambiguity**.

---

## 2. Framing and Semantic Drift

Clinical language is not neutral. Terms like “routine,” “minor,” or “conservative” carry implicit frames that shape decisions.

* To a surgeon, “conservative” may mean *non-operative management with uncertain long-term efficacy*
* To a patient, it may mean *safe, standard, low-risk care*

This divergence is not trivial—it directly alters decision outcomes. The same option, differently understood, produces different choices. This is not merely a communication failure; it is a **model mismatch**.

---

## 3. The Missing Variable: Weights

Clinical decision-making is typically presented as:

* Risks (probabilities of adverse outcomes)
* Benefits (probabilities of improvement)

But this is incomplete. What is missing is **how much each outcome matters to the patient**.

Formally, decisions depend on a composite structure:

[
L_{total} = \sum_i w_i \cdot L_i
]

Where:

* (L_i): specific outcomes (e.g., paralysis, pain relief, mortality)
* (w_i): patient-specific weights (e.g., value placed on independence vs survival)

Two patients may face identical probabilities yet choose differently because their (w_i) differ. Medicine rarely elicits these weights explicitly. Instead, it implicitly assumes them—often defaulting to survival or technical success.

This is the core failure: **the loss function is specified by the clinician, not co-constructed with the patient**.

---

## 4. When Misalignment Becomes Catastrophic

In cases of severe adverse outcomes—such as paralysis after surgery—the consequences of misalignment become stark.

The issue is not only that the outcome is poor, but that:

* The patient or family did not anticipate it
* The probability and implications were not internalized
* The decision did not reflect their actual values

This creates a rupture:

* Between expectation and reality
* Between institutional logic and personal meaning

Such ruptures erode trust and, in extreme cases, provoke profound emotional and social consequences.

---

## 5. The Surgeon’s Responsibility: Expanding Attention

Patients naturally focus on immediate suffering:

* pain
* disability
* fear

It is the clinician’s role to expand the frame of attention:

* long-term outcomes
* rare but severe risks
* tradeoffs across time and function

However, expanding attention is not sufficient. The clinician must also:

1. Ensure comprehension (not just disclosure)
2. Translate probabilities into lived realities
3. Help the patient articulate their own weights

Only then can a meaningful decision emerge.

---

## 6. From Disclosure to Alignment

A more rigorous model of consent requires three components:

### 1. Structured Alternatives

Clear, mutually understood options:

* Surgical
* Conservative
* No intervention

### 2. Quantified Outcomes

Not vague risks, but:

* Probabilities
* Time horizons
* Functional consequences

### 3. Explicit Weighting

Patients must be guided to answer:

* What matters most—longevity, mobility, independence, or comfort?
* What outcomes are unacceptable, regardless of probability?

Without this third step, the first two are insufficient.

---

## 7. Implications for Risk Models and Decision Tools

Risk calculators (e.g., for mortality, ESRD, hospitalization) are increasingly used in clinical care. However, they typically output probabilities without incorporating patient-specific weights.

This leads to a paradox:

> More precise numbers do not produce better decisions if the objective function remains undefined.

Future tools must therefore:

* Pair risk estimates with **preference elicitation**
* Allow dynamic weighting of outcomes
* Present tradeoffs in a way that reflects individual priorities

---

## 8. Conclusion: Consent as Shared Optimization

Informed consent should not be viewed as a legal checkpoint or informational transfer. It is a **joint optimization problem**:

* The physician contributes: data, probabilities, and unseen consequences
* The patient contributes: values, preferences, and lived context

The goal is not agreement for its own sake, but **alignment of the decision with the patient’s true objective function**.

When this alignment is absent, consent is nominal.
When it is present, even adverse outcomes remain intelligible within a shared framework.

---

## Final Thesis

The failure of informed consent in modern medicine is not primarily informational—it is structural.
It arises from **misaligned models of reality**.

The solution is not more disclosure, but **explicit co-construction of the loss function** that governs the decision itself.
