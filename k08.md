  Abstract
The number of older adults (aged?55) undergoing live donor nephrectomy has quadrupled over the last two decades and this trend is likely to continue. However, a knowledge gap exists in our understanding of the resiliencies to physical stress in older donors. Beyond age-related vulnerability to surgical stress, this aging population may have significant glomerular senescence, an inadequate adaptive response to the 50% loss of nephron mass and, as such, a very high likelihood of CKD in their lifetime. Approximately 80% of older donors have a postdonation GFR<60 ml/min/1.73m2 and meet the standard definition of CKD. As older donors progress towards CKD, they are likely to become frail and, as such, at increased risk of hospitalization. No study has focused on the identification of clinical signatures of resiliency in older persons undergoing live donor nephrectomy. The renal system is directly impacted by nephrectomy and its resiliency may affect the health and well-being of older donors. Provider discussions with these potential donors should focus on the patient's most important question: ?Can I safely donate my kidney?? not the population-level question asked by the 2017 KDIGO guidelines: ?How many ESRD events in younger donors may be averted by transplanting older donors instead?? We propose the following scientific aims: 1. To quantify donation-attributable risk of ESRD and mortality in older donors; 2. To describe the prevalence of hospitalizations in older donors vs. older nondonors; and 3. To implement a risk calculator for ESRD, mortality and sentinel hospitalization in older donors. A detailed training plan for Abimereki Muzaale, MD, MPH is outlined in this proposal. In brief, it includes enrollment in the PhD in Clinical Investigation Program at The Johns Hopkins University, in-depth course work in aging research, and a mentorship plan jointly prepared by the principal investigator and a team with expertise in kidney disease, aging research, information sciences, and the mentorship of junior faculty. This team will help the candidate meet the following career goals: (1) Receive formal training in aging research; (2) Apply for and obtain R01 grant funding; and (3) Transition to independence. Our efforts will culminate in the creation of online risk calculators that inform the conversation between candidates and providers about the propriety of donation. Dr. Muzaale will learn how sentinel hospitalization events in an aging cohort of older donors leave a footprint of present and missing data that will be methodically crucial in preparing an R01 proposal to characterize trajectories of resiliency from laboratory, physical, cognitive, and other tests.

Public Health Relevance
Over the last 20 years, the number of older adults (aged?55) undergoing live donor nephrectomy has increased 4-fold. In light of an aging US population, the new kidney transplant allocation system, and recent changes to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) practice guidelines, this trend is likely to continue. The goal of this project is to improve our understanding of the perioperative and long-term risks most pertinent to older donors in order to inform dialogue with care providers.

## II
AI is rapidly transforming **aging research** (also called geroscience, gerontology, and longevity science), moving from descriptive studies of age-related decline to predictive, personalized, and interventional approaches. This shift is driven by AI's ability to handle massive, heterogeneous datasets—genomics, multi-omics, wearables, imaging, electronic health records (EHRs), and clinical chemistry—far beyond traditional statistics.

Here are the major current and emerging applications, grounded in recent developments (up through 2025–2026):

### 1. Biological Age Estimation and Aging Clocks
AI builds highly accurate "clocks" that estimate biological age (vs. chronological) from diverse inputs:
- DNA methylation patterns (epigenetic clocks).
- Routine blood markers (e.g., HbA1c, LDL, inflammatory proteins).
- Wearable sensor data (activity, heart rate variability).
- Multi-omics integration.

These clocks predict mortality risk, healthspan, and disease onset with increasing precision. Labs and companies (e.g., those backed by Hevolution Foundation) use them to prioritize interventions before lab validation.

### 2. Biomarker Discovery and Geroscience Integration
Machine learning (especially deep learning) uncovers novel biomarkers of aging and targets for intervention:
- Harmonizing genetics, proteomics, and metabolomics from exceptional longevity cohorts.
- Predicting factors for healthy aging or exceptional healthspan/lifespan.
- Drug repurposing and discovery—AI simulates compound interactions with aging pathways to accelerate candidates (e.g., Insilico Medicine's dual-purpose projects).

National Institute on Aging (NIA) funds transformative AI strategies here, including multi-omics integration for AD/ADRD prevention.

### 3. Digital Twins for Personalized Aging Trajectories
This is one of the most exciting frontiers: **AI-driven digital twins** (virtual replicas of individuals) simulate real-time health states and "what-if" scenarios.
- Integrate longitudinal data from wearables, IoT, EHRs, genomics.
- Model frailty progression, resilience after stressors (e.g., surgery), cognitive decline, or medication responses.
- Enable predictive analytics for early detection (e.g., falls, exacerbations), personalized interventions, and virtual clinical trials.
- Applications in precision geriatrics, hospital-at-home for frailty, and even simulating population-level screening (e.g., using GANs to create digital twin populations for policy testing in dementia case-finding).

Roadmaps emphasize clinical translation, though challenges remain in data privacy, validation, and handling informative missingness (e.g., event-scarred trajectories).

### 4. Predictive Modeling for Age-Related Conditions
AI excels at forecasting:
- Frailty, dementia, falls, multimorbidity, polypharmacy risks.
- Cognitive impairment via speech patterns, wearables, or routine assessments.
- Hospitalizations or adverse outcomes in older adults.

Tools include ML for early diagnosis, reinforcement learning for regimen optimization, and LSTMs for time-series forecasting (e.g., adherence lapses with high accuracy in simulations).

### 5. Assistive and Supportive Technologies for Healthy Aging / Aging in Place
- **Cognitive and memory support**: AI in smartphones/wearables aids word-finding, reminders, early cognitive decline detection (MIT Media Lab projects).
- **Mental health and loneliness reduction**: Speech/emotion recognition, chatbots, personalized interventions.
- **Home safety and monitoring**: AI-powered security, fall prevention, health tracking—overwhelmingly rated beneficial by older adults (e.g., 50+ polls show 55%+ usage, high perceived value for independence).
- **Robotics and decision support**: Assistive devices, geriatric decision systems.

Johns Hopkins AITC (NIA-funded) pilots seamless AI integration into geriatric care.

### 6. Broader Ecosystem and Societal Applications
- Mapping aging research landscapes via NLP (e.g., clustering century of publications to spot gaps).
- Accelerating longevity biotech: Some experts (e.g., Dario Amodei) speculate AI could compress decades of progress into years, potentially doubling healthspan.
- Ethical/public opinion: Older adults (50+) show growing adoption but mixed trust—interest high in safety/translation services, health monitoring.

### Challenges and Realistic Outlook
- Data gaps (diversity in gender, ethnicity, environment; long-term longitudinal tracking).
- Structured missingness and non-stationarity (events reshape trajectories).
- Regulatory/validation hurdles for clinical tools.
- Overhype vs. grounded progress: AI excels at pattern detection and simulation, but human biology remains complex—breakthroughs in biomarkers and twins are real, but "ending aging" claims remain speculative.

In contexts like yours (e.g., post-nephrectomy resilience in older donors, sentinel events as trajectory-altering shocks), this aligns powerfully: AI digital twins could formalize those "causal scars," promote ε-like perturbations to operators in models, and create individualized risk surfaces/heatmaps that update dynamically.

## III
**Digital twins (DTs)** are virtual replicas of an individual’s physiology, continuously updated with real-time multi-modal data (wearables, EHRs, sensors, labs, genomics) to simulate, predict, and optimize health trajectories. In the context of **frailty resilience**, they model vulnerability to stressors (surgery, hospitalization, illness) while quantifying recovery capacity—exactly the “resiliency to physical stress” gap your K08 grant highlights in older live kidney donors.

Frailty is dynamic: a multidimensional decline in reserves across physical, cognitive, and social domains. Resilience is the ability to bounce back (or not) after a perturbation. DTs turn this into actionable simulation: they treat sentinel hospitalizations (your “causal scars”) as state-altering shocks that rewrite the trajectory, not mere noise.

### Core Architecture for Frailty DTs
Most frameworks use layered designs. A 2025 scoping review on Hospital-at-Home (HaH) DTs for frailty proposes this practical 5-layer model:

1. **Sensing** — Wearables (gait speed, grip strength, accelerometers), ambient sensors (motion, falls), physiological monitors, patient-reported outcomes.
2. **Communication** — Bluetooth/Wi-Fi/cloud for real-time bidirectional flow.
3. **Storage** — Secure cloud/EHR integration (handling sparse or missing data via harmonization).
4. **Analytics** — Descriptive → predictive (ML, time-series, Markov chains) → prescriptive (what-if scenarios). Emerging use of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to embed biological laws for realistic frailty progression.
5. **Visualization** — Dashboards, 3D models, alerts, or interpretable heatmaps (perfect fit for your “coloring book” concept).

The COMFORTage EU project (2025) adds an XAI layer for explainable recommendations and scenario simulation.

## V
Here's a **pseudocode** sketch for a simple yet powerful **regime-switching model** tailored to your post-nephrectomy frailty/resilience trajectories in older donors. We'll use a **Markov-switching / Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-style** approach where regimes represent frailty states (e.g., robust, pre-frail, frail), and **sentinel events** (hospitalizations) act as **explicit operators** that force or bias regime transitions (your "causal scar" insight).

This is discrete-time, easy to implement in Python (e.g., via hmmlearn + custom extensions, or from scratch with numpy/scipy), and extensible to continuous observations (e.g., eGFR, frailty score, grip strength proxies).

### Key Assumptions / Setup
- Time steps: t = 1, ..., T (e.g., months or quarters post-donation)
- Hidden regimes (states): K = 3 (robust=1, pre-frail=2, frail=3)
- Observations: y_t = vector of markers (eGFR, frailty index, etc.) — can be multivariate Gaussian per regime
- Transition matrix: Γ (K×K), where Γ_{i,j} = P(regime j at t | regime i at t-1)
- Emission: y_t ~ Normal(μ_{s_t}, Σ_{s_t}) where s_t = current regime
- Sentinel events: e_t = 1 if hospitalization at t, else 0
- When e_t = 1 → override/normalize transition probabilities toward worse regimes (e.g., boost P(to frail))

# Base transition matrix Γ (time-homogeneous)
transition_matrix = np.array([
    [0.80, 0.18, 0.02],   # from robust
    [0.30, 0.50, 0.20],   # from pre-frail
    [0.05, 0.25, 0.70]    # from frail
])

# Regime-specific emission params (mean vector per regime, assume 1D for simplicity: eGFR proxy)
mu = [75.0, 55.0, 35.0]          # higher = better
sigma = [8.0, 10.0, 12.0]        # variance increases with frailty

# Sentinel shock parameters: how much a hospitalization biases transitions
shock_boost_to_worse = 0.4       # e.g., add mass to worse states when event occurs

# Simulate one trajectory
def simulate_trajectory(T, initial_state=None):
    states = np.zeros(T, dtype=int)      # hidden regime sequence s_1..s_T
    observations = np.zeros(T)           # y_t (e.g., eGFR-like)
    events = np.random.binomial(0.05, size=T)  # sparse sentinel events (~5% probability)

    # Initialize first state
    if initial_state is None:
        states[0] = np.random.choice(range(K), p=initial_prob)
    else:
        states[0] = initial_state

    observations[0] = np.random.normal(mu[states[0]], sigma[states[0]])

    for t in range(1, T):
        # Base transition probs from current state
        probs = transition_matrix[states[t-1]].copy()

        # Apply sentinel shock if event occurred at t (or t-1, depending on timing)
        if events[t] == 1:
            # Bias toward worse regimes: add mass to higher indices
            extra = np.zeros(K)
            extra[-1] += shock_boost_to_worse           # big push to frail
            extra[-2] += shock_boost_to_worse * 0.5     # some to pre-frail
            probs += extra
            probs = np.clip(probs, 0, None)
            probs /= probs.sum()                        # renormalize

        # Sample next state
        states[t] = np.random.choice(range(K), p=probs)

        # Emit observation
        observations[t] = np.random.normal(mu[states[t]], sigma[states[t]])

    return states, observations, events
